Year 9 · Algebra

Factorising

What you'll learn

  • I can explain that factorising is the reverse of expanding.
  • I can factorise an expression by taking out the highest common factor, including letters.
  • I can factorise quadratics such as \(x^2 + 7x + 12\) by finding two numbers that multiply and add.
  • I can factorise quadratics with negative terms.
  • I can recognise and factorise the difference of two squares and perfect squares.
  • I can factorise fully, and use factorising to solve problems.

Key words

Factor
Something that divides exactly into a number or term. 3 and \(x\) are both factors of \(3x\).
Factorise
Write an expression as a product: something multiplied by something. It is the reverse of expanding.
Common factor
A factor shared by every term in an expression.
Highest common factor (HCF)
The biggest factor shared by every term. For \(12x\) and \(18x^2\) it is \(6x\).
Factorise fully
Keep factorising until nothing more can be taken out.
Quadratic expression
An expression where the highest power is \(x^2\), such as \(x^2 + 7x + 12\).
Difference of two squares
One square take away another, such as \(x^2 - 25\).

1. What factorising means

When you expand, you multiply out brackets. When you factorise, you go the other way: you put the brackets back in.

A factorised expression is a product: one thing multiplied by another. Factorised forms are useful because they show the building blocks of an expression, in the same way that \(12 = 3 \times 4\) shows the building blocks of 12. You will use factorising to simplify expressions, solve equations and sketch graphs.

Key idea

You can always check a factorisation by expanding it again. If you get back to where you started, it is right.

2. Taking out a common number

Look at \(6x + 15\). Both terms are in the 3 times table: \(6x = 3 \times 2x\) and \(15 = 3 \times 5\). So 3 is a common factor, and you can write it outside a bracket.

Method
  1. Find the highest common factor (HCF) of all the terms.
  2. Write it in front of a bracket.
  3. Divide each term by the HCF to find what goes inside the bracket.
  4. Check by expanding.

A number factor

Factorise \(20 - 8y\).

Show solution
  1. The HCF of 20 and 8 is 4.
  2. \(20 \div 4 = 5\) and \(8y \div 4 = 2y\).
  3. Check: \(4 \times 5 - 4 \times 2y = 20 - 8y\). ✓

\(20 - 8y = 4(5 - 2y)\)

Factorising fully

Factorise \(12x + 18\) fully.

Show solution
  1. 2 is a common factor: \(12x + 18 = 2(6x + 9)\). But 6 and 9 still share a factor of 3, so this is not fully factorised.
  2. The highest common factor of 12 and 18 is 6: \(12x \div 6 = 2x\), \(18 \div 6 = 3\).

\(12x + 18 = 6(2x + 3)\)

Check the bracket: if the terms inside still share a factor, you have not finished.

Quick check
  1. \(5x + 20\)
  2. \(14a - 21\)
  3. \(9 + 12t\)
Show answers
  1. \(5(x + 4)\)
  2. \(7(2a - 3)\)
  3. \(3(3 + 4t)\)

3. Common factors with letters

Letters can be common factors too. In \(x^2 + 5x\), both terms contain \(x\): \(x^2 = x \times x\) and \(5x = 5 \times x\). So

\[ x^2 + 5x = x(x + 5) \]

To find the HCF of terms with letters, find the HCF of the numbers, then include every letter that appears in all the terms, to the lowest power it appears.

Numbers and letters

Factorise \(12ab - 8a\) fully.

Show solution
  1. HCF of the numbers 12 and 8: 4.
  2. Letters in both terms: \(a\) (only the first term has \(b\)). So the HCF is \(4a\).
  3. Divide: \(12ab \div 4a = 3b\) and \(8a \div 4a = 2\).

\(12ab - 8a = 4a(3b - 2)\)

Squared terms

Factorise \(6x^2 + 9x\) fully.

Show solution
  1. HCF of 6 and 9: 3. Both terms contain at least one \(x\). HCF: \(3x\).
  2. \(6x^2 \div 3x = 2x\) and \(9x \div 3x = 3\).
  3. Check: \(3x \times 2x + 3x \times 3 = 6x^2 + 9x\). ✓

\(6x^2 + 9x = 3x(2x + 3)\)

Three terms, and a term that is all factor

Factorise (a) \(5p^2 - 10p + 15pq\)   (b) \(7x + 7\).

Show solution
  1. HCF of 5, 10 and 15 is 5; every term has \(p\). HCF \(= 5p\). Dividing each term: \(p\), \(-2\), \(+3q\). So \(5p^2 - 10p + 15pq = 5p(p - 2 + 3q)\).
  2. HCF \(= 7\). \(7x \div 7 = x\) and \(7 \div 7 = 1\). So \(7x + 7 = 7(x + 1)\). Don't leave out the 1: \(7 \times 1 = 7\).
Quick check
  1. \(y^2 - 7y\)
  2. \(8m^2 + 12m\)
  3. \(10xy + 15x\)
  4. \(6a + 6\)
Show answers
  1. \(y(y - 7)\)
  2. \(4m(2m + 3)\)
  3. \(5x(2y + 3)\)
  4. \(6(a + 1)\)

4. Factorising quadratics

A quadratic such as \(x^2 + 8x + 15\) has no common factor, but it can often be written as two brackets. Remember from Expanding two brackets:

\[ (x + a)(x + b) = x^2 + (a + b)x + ab \]

So to go backwards, you need two numbers that add to the number in front of \(x\), and multiply to the number on the end.

Rule

To factorise \(x^2 + bx + c\), find two numbers that multiply to \(c\) and add to \(b\). If the numbers are \(m\) and \(n\):

\[ x^2 + bx + c = (x + m)(x + n) \]

The easiest way to find the numbers is to list the factor pairs of \(c\), then check which pair adds to \(b\).

Listing factor pairs

Factorise \(x^2 + 7x + 12\).

Show solution

\(3 \times 4\)7 ✓

\(x^2 + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)\)

Check: \((x + 3)(x + 4) = x^2 + 4x + 3x + 12 = x^2 + 7x + 12\). ✓

A bigger number

Factorise \(x^2 + 10x + 16\).

Show solution
  1. Factor pairs of 16: \(1 \times 16\) (adds to 17), \(2 \times 8\) (adds to 10 ✓), \(4 \times 4\) (adds to 8).

\(x^2 + 10x + 16 = (x + 2)(x + 8)\)

Quick check
  1. \(x^2 + 6x + 8\)
  2. \(x^2 + 9x + 20\)
  3. \(x^2 + 11x + 10\)
Show answers
  1. \((x + 2)(x + 4)\)
  2. \((x + 4)(x + 5)\)
  3. \((x + 1)(x + 10)\)

5. Quadratics with negative terms

The same method works when some terms are negative, but now the factor pairs can include negative numbers. The signs in the quadratic tell you what to look for.

Both negative

Factorise \(x^2 - 9x + 14\).

Show solution
  1. Multiply to \(+14\), add to \(-9\). The product is positive and the sum is negative, so both numbers are negative.
  2. Negative pairs of 14: \(-1 \times -14\) (adds to \(-15\)), \(-2 \times -7\) (adds to \(-9\) ✓).

\(x^2 - 9x + 14 = (x - 2)(x - 7)\)

A negative last term

Factorise \(x^2 + 2x - 15\).

Show solution

\(x^2 + 2x - 15 = (x - 3)(x + 5)\)

Negative middle and last terms

Factorise \(x^2 - 4x - 21\).

Show solution
  1. Multiply to \(-21\), so one number is positive and one is negative.
  2. Add to \(-4\), so the bigger number is negative: \(-7\) and \(3\). Check: \(-7 \times 3 = -21\) ✓, \(-7 + 3 = -4\) ✓.

\(x^2 - 4x - 21 = (x - 7)(x + 3)\)

Quick check
  1. \(x^2 - 9x + 18\)
  2. \(x^2 + 3x - 10\)
  3. \(x^2 - x - 12\)
Show answers
  1. \((x - 3)(x - 6)\)
  2. \((x + 5)(x - 2)\)
  3. \((x - 4)(x + 3)\)

6. The difference of two squares

\(x^2 - 49\) has no middle term. Think of it as \(x^2 + 0x - 49\): you need two numbers that multiply to \(-49\) and add to \(0\). They are \(7\) and \(-7\).

\[ x^2 - 49 = (x + 7)(x - 7) \]
Difference of two squares \[ x^2 - a^2 = (x + a)(x - a) \]

Look for two terms that are both perfect squares, with a minus sign between them.

Spotting squares

Factorise (a) \(y^2 - 1\)   (b) \(100 - x^2\).

Show solution
  1. \(1 = 1^2\), so \(y^2 - 1 = (y + 1)(y - 1)\).
  2. \(100 = 10^2\), so \(100 - x^2 = (10 + x)(10 - x)\).
Sum of two squares

\(x^2 + 16\) does not factorise. No two numbers multiply to \(+16\) and add to \(0\).

Quick check
  1. \(x^2 - 16\)
  2. \(x^2 - 1\)
  3. \(81 - y^2\)
Show answers
  1. \((x + 4)(x - 4)\)
  2. \((x + 1)(x - 1)\)
  3. \((9 + y)(9 - y)\)

7. Perfect squares

Some quadratics factorise into the same bracket twice. You learnt that \((x + a)^2 = x^2 + 2ax + a^2\). So if the last term is a square number, and the middle number is double its square root, it is a perfect square.

Perfect squares

Factorise (a) \(x^2 + 6x + 9\)   (b) \(x^2 - 10x + 25\).

Show solution
  1. \(9 = 3^2\) and \(6 = 2 \times 3\). The numbers are 3 and 3: \(x^2 + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)^2\).
  2. \(25 = 5^2\) and \(-10 = 2 \times (-5)\). The numbers are \(-5\) and \(-5\): \(x^2 - 10x + 25 = (x - 5)^2\).
Quick check
  1. \(x^2 + 14x + 49\)
  2. \(x^2 - 2x + 1\)
  3. \(x^2 + 12x + 36\)
Show answers
  1. \((x + 7)^2\)
  2. \((x - 1)^2\)
  3. \((x + 6)^2\)

8. Factorising fully

Sometimes you need to use more than one method. Always take out a common factor first; what is left is often easier to factorise.

Factorising checklist
  1. Is there a common factor? If so, take it out.
  2. Count the terms left in the bracket.
    • Two terms with a minus between two squares? Difference of two squares.
    • Three terms like \(x^2 + bx + c\)? Find two numbers that multiply to \(c\) and add to \(b\).
  3. Check each bracket: can it be factorised again?
  4. Expand your answer to check it.

Common factor, then a quadratic

Factorise \(2x^2 + 10x + 12\) fully.

Show solution
  1. Common factor 2: \(2(x^2 + 5x + 6)\).
  2. Two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to 5: 2 and 3. So \(x^2 + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3)\).

\(2x^2 + 10x + 12 = 2(x + 2)(x + 3)\)

Common factor, then a difference of two squares

Factorise \(3x^2 - 27\) fully.

Show solution
  1. Common factor 3: \(3(x^2 - 9)\).
  2. \(x^2 - 9\) is a difference of two squares: \((x + 3)(x - 3)\).

\(3x^2 - 27 = 3(x + 3)(x - 3)\)

9. Using factorising

Factorising is not just an algebra exercise. It can make arithmetic quicker and help you find unknown lengths.

Quick arithmetic

Work out, without a calculator: (a) \(23 \times 47 + 23 \times 53\)   (b) \(53^2 - 47^2\).

Show solution
  1. 23 is a common factor: \(23(47 + 53) = 23 \times 100 = 2300\).
  2. Difference of two squares: \((53 + 47)(53 - 47) = 100 \times 6 = 600\).

Finding a missing side

A rectangle has an area of \(x^2 + 9x + 20\) cm². Its length is \((x + 5)\) cm. Find its width, and find both lengths when \(x = 6\).

Show solution
  1. Factorise the area: two numbers that multiply to 20 and add to 9 are 4 and 5. So \(x^2 + 9x + 20 = (x + 4)(x + 5)\).
  2. One side is \(x + 5\), so the other is \(x + 4\).
  3. When \(x = 6\): length 11 cm and width 10 cm.
  4. Check: area \(= 11 \times 10 = 110\), and \(6^2 + 9 \times 6 + 20 = 36 + 54 + 20 = 110\). ✓

Width \((x + 4)\) cm; when \(x = 6\) the rectangle is 11 cm by 10 cm.

Common mistakes

Not factorising fully

\(12x + 16 = 2(6x + 8)\) is true, but not finished: \(6x + 8\) still has a common factor. Use the highest common factor: \(4(3x + 4)\).

Losing the 1

\(7p - 7\) is \(7(p - 1)\), not \(7p\) or \(7(p)\). When a whole term is taken out, a 1 is left behind.

Signs the wrong way round

\(x^2 - x - 12\) is \((x - 4)(x + 3)\), not \((x + 4)(x - 3)\), which expands to \(x^2 + x - 12\). Always check the sum, including its sign.

Factorising a sum of squares

\(x^2 + 16\) cannot be factorised. Only a difference of two squares can.

Summary

  • Factorising is the reverse of expanding. Check every answer by expanding.
  • Take out the highest common factor first, including any letters shared by every term.
  • \(x^2 + bx + c = (x + m)(x + n)\), where \(m\) and \(n\) multiply to \(c\) and add to \(b\).
  • The signs of \(b\) and \(c\) tell you the signs of \(m\) and \(n\).
  • \(x^2 - a^2 = (x + a)(x - a)\): the difference of two squares.
  • \(x^2 + 2ax + a^2 = (x + a)^2\): a perfect square.
  • "Factorise fully" means keep going until no bracket can be factorised again.
