Quadratic graphs

What you'll learn

  • I can draw a parabola from a table of values.
  • I can name the key features of a parabola: turning point, axis of symmetry, \(x\)-intercepts and \(y\)-intercept.
  • I can describe how changing \(a\), \(h\) and \(k\) in \(y = a(x - h)^2 + k\) moves and stretches the graph.
  • I can sketch a parabola from turning-point form, factorised form or expanded form.
  • I can write the equation of a parabola from its graph.
  • I can use quadratic graphs to solve problems in context.

Key words

Quadratic
An expression or equation where the highest power of \(x\) is \(x^2\), such as \(y = x^2 - 2x - 3\).
Parabola
The U-shaped (or upside-down U-shaped) curve that is the graph of a quadratic.
Turning point
The lowest point (minimum) or highest point (maximum) of a parabola. Also called the vertex.
Axis of symmetry
The vertical mirror line through the turning point.
\(x\)-intercept (root)
A point where the graph crosses the \(x\)-axis, where \(y = 0\).
\(y\)-intercept
The point where the graph crosses the \(y\)-axis, where \(x = 0\).

1. The shape of a parabola

The simplest quadratic graph is \(y = x^2\). Make a table of values by squaring each \(x\) value. Remember that squaring a negative number gives a positive answer.

Table of values for \(y = x^2\)
\(x\)−3−2−10123
\(y\)9410149

Plot the points and join them with a smooth curve, not straight lines. Here is \(y = x^2\) together with \(y = -x^2\).

\(y = x^2\)\(y = -x^2\)

Key idea

Every quadratic graph is a parabola. It is symmetrical about a vertical line.

If the \(x^2\) term is positive the parabola opens upwards, ∪, and has a minimum. If the \(x^2\) term is negative it opens downwards, ∩, and has a maximum.

Drawing tips
  • Use the symmetry: the \(y\) values repeat either side of the turning point. If they don't, check your arithmetic.
  • Draw one smooth curve through all the points. The bottom should be rounded, not pointed.
  • Don't join the end points to the edge of the grid with straight lines; just stop the curve.
  • Label the curve with its equation.

4. Moving and stretching \(y = x^2\)

Every parabola is the graph of \(y = x^2\) moved, stretched or flipped. Learning how the numbers in the equation do this lets you sketch a graph without a table of values.

Adding a number: \(y = x^2 + k\)

\(y = x^2\)\(y = x^2 + 3\)\(y = x^2 - 2\)

Adding \(k\) moves the whole graph up \(k\) units (or down, if \(k\) is negative). The turning point moves from \((0, 0)\) to \((0, k)\).

Multiplying by a number: \(y = ax^2\)

\(y = x^2\)\(y = 2x^2\)\(y = \tfrac{1}{2}x^2\)\(y = -x^2\)

The number \(a\) in front of \(x^2\) changes the steepness. A bigger number makes the parabola narrower; a number between 0 and 1 makes it wider. A negative \(a\) flips it upside down.

Brackets: \(y = (x - h)^2\)

Replacing \(x\) with \((x - h)\) moves the graph sideways. For example \(y = (x - 3)^2\) is \(y = x^2\) moved 3 units to the right, because \(y = 0\) when \(x = 3\). Watch the sign: \(y = (x + 3)^2\) moves 3 units to the left.

Putting it together: turning-point form

Turning-point form \[ y = a(x - h)^2 + k \]

has its turning point at \((h, k)\) and axis of symmetry \(x = h\). It opens upwards if \(a > 0\) and downwards if \(a < 0\).

  • Find settings where the graph has no \(x\)-intercepts. What do you notice about the signs of \(a\) and \(k\)?

Reading turning-point form

Write down the turning point of each parabola, and say whether it is a maximum or a minimum.

  1. \(y = (x - 5)^2 + 1\)
  2. \(y = (x + 2)^2 - 7\)
  3. \(y = -3(x - 1)^2 + 4\)
Show solution
  1. \(h = 5\), \(k = 1\): turning point \((5, 1)\). \(a = 1 > 0\), so a minimum.
  2. \((x + 2) = (x - (-2))\), so \(h = -2\), \(k = -7\): turning point \((-2, -7)\), a minimum.
  3. \(h = 1\), \(k = 4\): turning point \((1, 4)\). \(a = -3 < 0\), so a maximum.

5. Sketching from turning-point form

A sketch shows the shape and the key points, labelled with their coordinates. It does not need to be drawn on graph paper.

Method
  1. Read off the turning point \((h, k)\).
  2. Decide if it opens up (\(a > 0\)) or down (\(a < 0\)).
  3. Find the \(y\)-intercept by putting \(x = 0\).
  4. Find any \(x\)-intercepts by putting \(y = 0\) and solving.
  5. Sketch a smooth, symmetrical curve through the points and label them.

Opening upwards

Sketch \(y = (x + 2)^2 - 1\).

Show solution
  1. Turning point: \((-2, -1)\). \(a = 1\), so it opens upwards.
  2. \(y\)-intercept: \(y = (0 + 2)^2 - 1 = 3\).
  3. \(x\)-intercepts: \((x + 2)^2 - 1 = 0\), so \((x + 2)^2 = 1\), \(x + 2 = \pm 1\), giving \(x = -1\) and \(x = -3\).

Opening downwards

Sketch \(y = -(x - 3)^2 + 4\).

Show solution
  1. Turning point: \((3, 4)\). \(a = -1\) is negative, so it opens downwards and \((3, 4)\) is a maximum.
  2. \(y\)-intercept: \(y = -(0 - 3)^2 + 4 = -9 + 4 = -5\).
  3. \(x\)-intercepts: \((x - 3)^2 = 4\), so \(x - 3 = \pm 2\), giving \(x = 1\) and \(x = 5\).

No \(x\)-intercepts

Sketch \(y = (x - 1)^2 + 2\).

Show solution
  1. Turning point: \((1, 2)\), a minimum.
  2. \(y\)-intercept: \(y = (0 - 1)^2 + 2 = 3\).
  3. The lowest point is at \(y = 2\), above the \(x\)-axis, so the graph never crosses it. There are no \(x\)-intercepts. (Trying to solve \((x - 1)^2 = -2\) fails, because no square is negative.)
  4. Use symmetry for one more point: \((0, 3)\) is 1 unit left of the axis \(x = 1\), so \((2, 3)\) is also on the graph.

6. Sketching from factorised form

In factorised form you can read the \(x\)-intercepts straight from the brackets. If \(y = (x - p)(x - q)\), then \(y = 0\) when \(x = p\) or \(x = q\).

Factorised form \[ y = a(x - p)(x - q) \]

crosses the \(x\)-axis at \(x = p\) and \(x = q\). The axis of symmetry is halfway between: \(x = \dfrac{p + q}{2}\).

Explore

Drag \(p\) and \(q\) to move the \(x\)-intercepts, and \(a\) to stretch or flip the graph. Watch the equation and the turning point change.

  • What happens when \(p = q\)?
  • Keep \(p\) and \(q\) fixed and change \(a\). Which features stay the same?

Sketching from factorised form

Sketch \(y = (x - 2)(x + 4)\). Label the intercepts and the turning point.

Show solution
  1. \(x\)-intercepts: set each bracket to zero. \(x - 2 = 0\) gives \(x = 2\); \(x + 4 = 0\) gives \(x = -4\).
  2. \(y\)-intercept: put \(x = 0\). \(y = (0 - 2)(0 + 4) = -8\).
  3. Axis of symmetry: halfway between the roots. \(x = \dfrac{2 + (-4)}{2} = -1\).
  4. Turning point: put \(x = -1\). \(y = (-1 - 2)(-1 + 4) = (-3)(3) = -9\), so \((-1, -9)\).
  5. The \(x^2\) term is positive, so it opens upwards.

A negative \(a\)

Sketch \(y = -2x(x - 3)\).

Show solution
  1. \(x\)-intercepts: \(-2x = 0\) gives \(x = 0\); \(x - 3 = 0\) gives \(x = 3\).
  2. \(y\)-intercept: \(0\) (it passes through the origin).
  3. Axis of symmetry: \(x = 1.5\). Turning point: \(y = -2(1.5)(1.5 - 3) = -2(1.5)(-1.5) = 4.5\), so \((1.5, 4.5)\).
  4. \(a = -2\) is negative, so it opens downwards and \((1.5, 4.5)\) is a maximum.

7. Sketching from expanded form

In expanded form, \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\), you can read the \(y\)-intercept straight away: it is \(c\). To find the rest, factorise first.

Three ways to write \(y = x^2 - 2x - 3\)
FormEquationRead off
Expanded\(y = x^2 - 2x - 3\)\(y\)-intercept: \(-3\)
Factorised\(y = (x + 1)(x - 3)\)\(x\)-intercepts: \(-1\) and \(3\)
Turning point\(y = (x - 1)^2 - 4\)turning point: \((1, -4)\)

Factorise first

Sketch \(y = x^2 + 2x - 8\).

Show solution
  1. \(y\)-intercept: \(c = -8\).
  2. Factorise: two numbers that multiply to \(-8\) and add to \(2\) are \(4\) and \(-2\). So \(y = (x + 4)(x - 2)\).
  3. \(x\)-intercepts: \(x = -4\) and \(x = 2\).
  4. Axis of symmetry: \(x = \dfrac{-4 + 2}{2} = -1\).
  5. Turning point: \(y = (-1)^2 + 2(-1) - 8 = 1 - 2 - 8 = -9\), so \((-1, -9)\).

Intercepts \((-4, 0)\), \((2, 0)\), \((0, -8)\); turning point \((-1, -9)\), a minimum.

Extension

For \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\), the axis of symmetry is always \(x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}\). Check: for \(y = x^2 + 2x - 8\), \(x = -\dfrac{2}{2} = -1\). ✓ This works even when the quadratic does not factorise.

8. Finding the equation from a graph

Choose the form that matches the information on the graph:

  • If you know the \(x\)-intercepts, use \(y = a(x - p)(x - q)\).
  • If you know the turning point, use \(y = a(x - h)^2 + k\).

Then use one more point on the graph (often the \(y\)-intercept) to find \(a\).

From the intercepts

Find the equation of this parabola.

Show solution
  1. The \(x\)-intercepts are \(-2\) and \(4\), so \(y = a(x + 2)(x - 4)\).
  2. Use the \(y\)-intercept to find \(a\). At \(x = 0\): \(-8 = a(2)(-4) = -8a\), so \(a = 1\).

\(y = (x + 2)(x - 4)\), or expanded, \(y = x^2 - 2x - 8\)

From the turning point

A parabola has its turning point at \((2, -3)\) and passes through \((0, 1)\). Find its equation.

Show solution
  1. Turning point \((2, -3)\): \(y = a(x - 2)^2 - 3\).
  2. Substitute \((0, 1)\): \(1 = a(0 - 2)^2 - 3 = 4a - 3\).
  3. \(4a = 4\), so \(a = 1\).

\(y = (x - 2)^2 - 3\)

When \(a\) is not 1

A parabola crosses the \(x\)-axis at \(1\) and \(5\), and the \(y\)-axis at \(-10\). Find its equation.

Show solution
  1. \(y = a(x - 1)(x - 5)\).
  2. At \(x = 0\): \(-10 = a(-1)(-5) = 5a\), so \(a = -2\).

\(y = -2(x - 1)(x - 5)\). It opens downwards.

9. Quadratic graphs in context

Anything thrown into the air follows a parabola. Quadratics also describe areas, profits and the shapes of bridges and satellite dishes.

A ball thrown upwards

A ball is thrown straight up. Its height \(h\) metres after \(t\) seconds is \(h = -5t^2 + 20t\).

  1. When does the ball land?
  2. What is its greatest height, and when does it reach it?
  3. When is the ball 15 m above the ground?
Show solution
  1. Factorise: \(h = -5t(t - 4)\). \(h = 0\) when \(t = 0\) (thrown) and \(t = 4\). It lands after 4 seconds.
  2. The maximum is halfway between \(t = 0\) and \(t = 4\), at \(t = 2\). \(h = -5(4) + 20(2) = 20\). Greatest height 20 m after 2 seconds.
  3. \(-5t^2 + 20t = 15\). Divide by \(-5\): \(t^2 - 4t = -3\), so \(t^2 - 4t + 3 = 0\), \((t - 1)(t - 3) = 0\). At 1 second (going up) and 3 seconds (coming down).

Maximum area

A farmer has 20 m of fencing to make a rectangular pen. If one side is \(x\) m, show that the area is \(A = x(10 - x)\), and find the largest possible area.

Show solution
  1. The perimeter is 20 m, so two sides add to 10 m. If one side is \(x\), the other is \(10 - x\). Area \(A = x(10 - x)\).
  2. This is a parabola with roots \(x = 0\) and \(x = 10\), opening downwards (the \(x^2\) term is \(-x^2\)).
  3. The maximum is halfway, at \(x = 5\): \(A = 5 \times 5 = 25\).

The largest area is 25 m², when the pen is a 5 m by 5 m square.

Common mistakes

Signs in the brackets

\(y = (x + 4)(x - 1)\) crosses the \(x\)-axis at \(x = -4\) and \(x = 1\): the signs swap. In the same way, \(y = (x - 3)^2 + 2\) has its turning point at \((3, 2)\), not \((-3, 2)\).

Ignoring \(a\)

A negative number in front of the \(x^2\) (or in front of the brackets) flips the parabola upside down, so the turning point is a maximum.

Squaring negatives

When \(x = -3\), \(x^2 = 9\), not \(-9\). On a calculator, type \((-3)^2\) with brackets.

Pointed bottoms

A parabola is rounded at its turning point. If your sketch looks like a V, you have joined points with straight lines.

Summary

  • The graph of a quadratic is a symmetrical curve called a parabola.
  • \(a > 0\): opens upwards with a minimum. \(a < 0\): opens downwards with a maximum.
  • Turning-point form \(y = a(x - h)^2 + k\): turning point \((h, k)\), axis \(x = h\).
  • Factorised form \(y = a(x - p)(x - q)\): \(x\)-intercepts \(p\) and \(q\), axis halfway between.
  • Expanded form \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\): \(y\)-intercept \(c\).
  • To find an equation from a graph, pick the matching form, then use another point to find \(a\).
